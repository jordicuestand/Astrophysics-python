# Hubble's Law: Measuring the Expansion of the Universe

## 1. Overview

This Python experiment illustrates **Hubble's law**, the empirical relation between the distance of a galaxy and its recession velocity:

\[
v = H_0 d
\]

where:

- \(v\) is the recession velocity of the galaxy,
- \(d\) is its distance,
- \(H_0\) is the present-day Hubble constant.

A perfectly homogeneous expanding Universe would produce a straight line in a velocity-distance diagram.

Real galaxies, however, also have **peculiar velocities** caused mainly by local gravitational interactions. For this reason, observed galaxies do not lie exactly on the Hubble line.

This simulation reproduces both effects:

1. the systematic recession caused by cosmic expansion;
2. random peculiar velocities superimposed on that expansion.

The program then tries to recover \(H_0\) from the simulated observations, in much the same conceptual way that astronomers infer cosmological parameters from observational data.

## 3. What the figure shows

The program generates two panels.

### Left panel — Hubble diagram

Each point represents a simulated galaxy.

The general trend follows

\[
v = H_0 d
\]

but the galaxies do not fall exactly on the line because a random peculiar velocity has been added to each one.

The shaded region represents a characteristic range of one standard deviation (\(\pm1\sigma\)) of the simulated peculiar velocities.

The important point is not that every galaxy follows Hubble's law exactly, but that the **global distance-velocity relation emerges from the population of galaxies**.

### Right panel — velocity residuals

For every galaxy, the program calculates

\[
\Delta v = v_{\rm observed} - v_{\rm predicted}.
\]

The dominant Hubble expansion has therefore been removed.

What remains is mainly the simulated peculiar velocity.

Notice that the vertical scale is much smaller than in the left panel. The right panel can be understood as a **magnified view of the velocity scatter around the Hubble relation**, after subtracting the fitted expansion.

This explains why the left panel may look very well aligned while the right panel appears highly scattered: the same deviations are being examined on a much finer velocity scale.

## 4. Parameters you can modify

The most interesting part of this experiment is changing the input parameters and observing what happens.

The main parameters are located near the beginning of the script:

```python
H0 = 70.0

n_galaxies = 50
min_distance = 5.0
max_distance = 200.0

peculiar_sigma = 250.0

random_seed = 42
```

### `H0` — Hubble constant

```python
H0 = 70.0
```

Units are:

```text
km/s/Mpc
```

A value of 70 means that, according to the simple Hubble law, increasing the distance by 1 Mpc increases the recession velocity by approximately 70 km/s.

For example:

| Distance | Hubble recession velocity |
|---:|---:|
| 1 Mpc | 70 km/s |
| 10 Mpc | 700 km/s |
| 50 Mpc | 3,500 km/s |
| 100 Mpc | 7,000 km/s |
| 200 Mpc | 14,000 km/s |

`H0 = 70` is a useful approximate value for this educational experiment.

Modern determinations of \(H_0\) depend on the observational method and cosmological inference used. Values around **67–74 km/s/Mpc** provide physically meaningful experiments.

Try, for example:

```python
H0 = 67.4
```

and:

```python
H0 = 73.0
```

These values approximately illustrate the well-known **Hubble tension** between determinations based on the early Universe and measurements based on the local astronomical distance scale.

This simulation does **not** attempt to resolve that tension. It simply allows the effect of different values of \(H_0\) to be explored.

---

### `peculiar_sigma` — peculiar velocities

```python
peculiar_sigma = 250.0
```

This parameter controls the standard deviation of the random peculiar velocities added to the Hubble expansion.

A peculiar velocity is a galaxy's motion relative to the ideal cosmological expansion.

For example, with:

```python
peculiar_sigma = 250.0
```

typical deviations are of the order of a few hundred km/s.

This is a reasonable order of magnitude for an educational simulation of galaxy peculiar motions, although real peculiar velocities depend strongly on environment. Galaxies belonging to groups and clusters can have substantially different velocity distributions.

Try:

```python
peculiar_sigma = 0.0
```

The points fall exactly on the Hubble line.

Then try:

```python
peculiar_sigma = 100.0
peculiar_sigma = 250.0
peculiar_sigma = 500.0
```

and compare the figures.

The cosmological expansion has not changed — only the local motions superimposed on it have changed.

## 5. A particularly important experiment: change the distance scale

Keep:

```python
H0 = 70.0
peculiar_sigma = 250.0
```

but change `max_distance`.

### Experiment A — nearby Universe

```python
max_distance = 20.0
```

At 20 Mpc, Hubble's law predicts only:

\[
v = 70 \times 20 = 1400\ {\rm km/s}.
\]

A peculiar velocity of a few hundred km/s is therefore a substantial fraction of the cosmological recession velocity.

The Hubble relation becomes noticeably noisy.

### Experiment B — larger scale

Now use:

```python
max_distance = 200.0
```

At 200 Mpc:

\[
v = 70 \times 200 = 14000\ {\rm km/s}.
\]

A peculiar velocity of 250 km/s is now relatively small compared with the recession velocity.

The global Hubble relation becomes much clearer.

### What have we learned?

This experiment illustrates an important physical idea:

> Hubble's law describes the large-scale expansion of the Universe. Local gravitational motions can dominate or significantly perturb the relation on smaller scales.

The peculiar velocities have not become smaller at large distances. Rather, the cosmological recession velocity has become much larger relative to them.

## 6. Estimating the Hubble constant

The program does not only draw the theoretical Hubble law.

It treats the simulated galaxies as if they were observations and estimates \(H_0\) from them.

The fitted model is

\[
v = H_{0,\rm fit} d.
\]

The line is constrained to pass through the origin, giving the least-squares estimate

\[
H_{0,\rm fit}
=
\frac{\sum_i d_i v_i}
     {\sum_i d_i^2}.
\]

The program prints both the value used to generate the artificial Universe and the value recovered from the simulated observations:

```text
Input H0:             70.00 km/s/Mpc
Estimated H0:         ...
Difference:           ... %
```

They will generally not be exactly equal because the simulated observations contain peculiar velocities.

This distinction is fundamental in observational science:

**a physical parameter is not normally read directly from one observation; it is inferred from data containing dispersion and uncertainty.**

## 7. Number of galaxies

Try changing:

```python
n_galaxies = 10
```

then:

```python
n_galaxies = 50
```

and finally:

```python
n_galaxies = 500
```

With only a few galaxies, random peculiar velocities can noticeably affect the fitted value of \(H_0\).

With many galaxies, positive and negative peculiar velocities tend to compensate statistically, and the estimated slope generally becomes more stable.

This is a simple illustration of why astronomical surveys benefit from large samples.

## 8. Random seed

```python
random_seed = 42
```

The peculiar velocities are generated randomly.

The random seed makes the experiment **reproducible**: every execution with the same parameters and seed generates the same artificial galaxy sample.

Try:

```python
random_seed = 1
random_seed = 10
random_seed = 100
```

The individual galaxies will change, and so will the estimated \(H_0\).

This is particularly interesting when `n_galaxies` is small.

## 9. Hubble radius

Once \(H_0\) has been estimated, the program calculates the Hubble radius:

\[
R_H = \frac{c}{H_0}.
\]

For approximately

\[
H_0 = 70\ {\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}},
\]

this gives roughly

\[
R_H \approx 4280\ {\rm Mpc},
\]

or about 14 billion light-years.

The Hubble radius is a characteristic cosmological distance scale.

### Important warning

The Hubble radius should **not** simply be interpreted as "the radius of the Universe".

Nor is it identical to the cosmological particle horizon or to the maximum distance from which we can currently receive light.

These concepts depend on the expansion history of the Universe.

---

## 10. Hubble time

The program also calculates:

\[
t_H = \frac{1}{H_0}.
\]

For:

\[
H_0 \approx 70\ {\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}},
\]

the result is approximately:

\[
t_H \approx 14\ {\rm billion\ years}.
\]

This is remarkably close to the actual age of the Universe.

However:

> The Hubble time is not, in general, exactly equal to the age of the Universe.

The equality would only follow directly from a simplified model in which the expansion rate behaved in a particular way.

In the real Universe, the expansion rate has evolved with cosmic time: radiation, matter and dark energy have dominated at different epochs.

This is the subject of the next cosmology experiment in this repository.

## 12. What is simplified in this model?

This is an educational simulation, not a precision cosmological analysis.

In particular:

- galaxy distances are generated artificially;
- peculiar velocities follow a simple Gaussian distribution;
- observational uncertainties in distance and velocity are ignored;
- selection effects are ignored;
- the linear relation \(v=H_0d\) is used;
- relativistic and high-redshift effects are not included;
- the evolution of the Hubble parameter with cosmic time is not included.

For sufficiently distant galaxies, cosmological redshift cannot simply be treated as an ordinary velocity obeying the non-relativistic relation used here.

The purpose of the simulation is therefore not precision cosmology, but understanding the physical and observational meaning of the local Hubble relation.
